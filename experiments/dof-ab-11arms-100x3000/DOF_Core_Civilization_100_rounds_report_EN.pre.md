**What happens when we change not how intelligent an AI agent is, but the fundamental principle guiding its decisions?**

Most AI agents today are designed to optimize a specific objective: win a game, accumulate resources, maximize productivity, or achieve the highest possible reward.

But what if we approached decision-making differently?

**DOF-Core (Degrees of Freedom Core)** is a decision-making framework I developed around a different objective: maximizing the future possibilities available to a system and all its participants.

Rather than pursuing immediate gains at any cost, a DOF-oriented agent aims to preserve autonomy, avoid irreversible losses, and expand the range of viable future paths for the entire system.

**But does this approach actually work?**

To explore that question, we conducted an experiment using the strategy game *Civilization*: 100 experimental rounds, 11 AI behavior configurations, ranging from zero DOF adoption to all civilizations following DOF principles.

In total, we analyzed 1,100 simulations, each running for 3,000 turns.

The results were striking.

Under standard AI behavior, an average of 5.69 out of 10 original civilizations survived. When all agents followed the DOF policy, all 10 civilizations survived in every one of the 100 games.

Their combined game score increased by almost 3.9 times, while recorded war declarations dropped to zero.

But there was also a surprising counterexample: a single DOF agent surrounded by standard AI opponents survived in only 18% of games.

**This reveals an intriguing paradox: the effectiveness of a decision-making principle may depend not only on an individual agent's behavior, but also on how widely that principle is adopted across the system.**

Of course, these results do not prove DOF-Core as a universal theory. Further experiments are needed to isolate the effects of individual policy rules, conflict prevention mechanisms, and game-specific dynamics.

Nevertheless, they raise a much broader question:

**What if the most effective long-term strategy for a society is not competition for limited resources, but the collective expansion of future possibilities?**

And could this principle become a foundation for building more stable, cooperative, and resilient multi-agent AI systems?
